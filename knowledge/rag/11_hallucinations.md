# Hallucinations

---

# What are Hallucinations?

In AI systems, hallucinations are outputs generated by a language model that:

* are false
* unsupported
* fabricated
* misleading
* inconsistent with evidence

while still sounding plausible and confident.

Hallucinations are one of the biggest challenges in modern LLM systems.

---

# Core Idea

A language model is fundamentally:

```text
a probabilistic next-token predictor
```

Its objective is not:

```text
truth
```

Its objective is:

```text
predicting likely token sequences
```

This distinction is extremely important.

LLMs generate text that:

* sounds coherent
* matches learned patterns
* appears plausible

but may not be factually correct.

---

# Why Hallucinations Happen

Hallucinations occur because LLMs:

* do not truly understand reality
* do not inherently verify facts
* operate probabilistically
* may lack necessary context
* may overgeneralize learned patterns

The model tries to generate:

```text
likely continuations
```

not guaranteed truths.

---

# Hallucinations Without Retrieval

Without retrieval systems, the model relies mainly on:

* training data
* statistical patterns
* prompt context

If the model lacks sufficient information:

* it may guess
* invent details
* produce fabricated reasoning

---

# Hallucinations in RAG Systems

RAG reduces hallucinations but does not eliminate them.

Even with retrieval, hallucinations may still occur because:

* retrieval failed
* retrieved context is incomplete
* retrieved context is ambiguous
* prompting is weak
* the model ignores evidence
* context is noisy

---

# Hallucinations vs Errors

Not every mistake is a hallucination.

A hallucination usually implies:

```text
confident unsupported generation
```

Examples:

* invented citations
* fabricated experiments
* fake numerical values
* unsupported scientific claims

---

# Example

Suppose retrieval returns:

```text
Experiment A:
Scintillation index = 0.31
```

If the model answers:

```text
Experiment A reached a scintillation index of 0.8
```

without evidence:

that is a hallucination.

---

# Types of Hallucinations

## 1. Fabricated Facts

Invented information.

Example:

* fake papers
* fake experiments
* nonexistent authors

---

## 2. Numerical Hallucinations

Incorrect numbers.

Especially dangerous in scientific systems.

Examples:

* wrong measurements
* invented statistics
* altered equations

---

## 3. Citation Hallucinations

Invented references or unsupported citations.

Example:

```text
"According to Smith et al. (2025)..."
```

when no such paper exists.

---

## 4. Reasoning Hallucinations

Incorrect logical conclusions.

Example:

* unsupported causal claims
* invalid scientific interpretations

---

## 5. Contextual Hallucinations

The model misinterprets retrieved context.

Example:

* mixing experiments
* confusing regimes
* merging unrelated evidence

---

# Hallucinations and Missing Context

One major cause:

```text
insufficient context
```

If relevant information is absent:

the model may attempt to fill gaps automatically.

This often produces plausible but false outputs.

---

# Hallucinations and Retrieval Failures

Weak retrieval strongly increases hallucinations.

Problems include:

* missing relevant chunks
* noisy retrieval
* irrelevant context
* poor chunking
* weak embeddings

Hallucinations are often retrieval problems in disguise.

---

# Hallucinations and Context Overload

Too much context can also increase hallucinations.

Why?

Because:

* important information gets buried
* retrieval noise increases
* the model becomes confused
* conflicting evidence appears

Good RAG systems maximize:

```text
signal / noise ratio
```

inside the prompt.

---

# Hallucinations and Prompting

Prompt design strongly affects hallucinations.

Weak prompts:

* encourage guessing
* allow unsupported claims
* fail to enforce grounding

Better prompts encourage:

* evidence-based reasoning
* uncertainty reporting
* explicit grounding

---

# Example Grounding Prompt

```text
Use only retrieved information.

If the answer is unsupported or uncertain, state the limitation clearly.

Do not invent facts.
```

This helps reduce hallucinations.

---

# Grounding

Grounding means:

```text
answers should be supported by evidence
```

Retrieved context acts as:

```text
external grounding source
```

Grounded systems hallucinate less.

---

# Faithfulness

Faithfulness measures:

```text
whether the answer remains faithful to retrieved evidence
```

A response may sound convincing while:

* exaggerating conclusions
* altering numbers
* introducing unsupported claims

Faithfulness is especially important in scientific systems.

---

# Hallucination Detection

Possible detection methods:

* citation verification
* fact checking
* retrieval validation
* cross-checking answers
* human review
* LLM-as-a-judge systems

No perfect hallucination detector currently exists.

---

# Retrieval-Augmented Grounding

RAG systems attempt to reduce hallucinations by:

```text
retrieving evidence before generation
```

Pipeline:

```text
Query
   ↓
Retrieval
   ↓
Evidence
   ↓
Grounded Generation
```

This is fundamentally more reliable than:

```text
pure memorization
```

---

# Why Hallucinations Are Dangerous

Hallucinations become especially dangerous in:

* medicine
* law
* engineering
* scientific research
* autonomous systems

because users may trust fluent outputs.

Fluency is not equivalent to correctness.

---

# Hallucinations in Scientific Systems

Scientific hallucinations are especially problematic because:

* numerical precision matters
* evidence matters
* citations matter
* reproducibility matters
* unsupported claims are dangerous

Examples:

* invented experimental behavior
* incorrect equations
* fake statistical conclusions
* altered turbulence metrics

---

# Hallucinations and Confidence

A major issue:

```text
LLMs often sound confident even when incorrect
```

Confidence is not a reliable indicator of truth.

This is one reason grounding is essential.

---

# Common Strategies to Reduce Hallucinations

## Better Retrieval

Improve:

* chunking
* embeddings
* reranking
* filtering

---

## Better Prompting

Encourage:

* grounding
* uncertainty reporting
* evidence usage

---

## Citation Requirements

Force the model to reference retrieved evidence.

---

## Smaller Cleaner Contexts

Reduce retrieval noise.

---

## Verification Systems

Add:

* fact checking
* validation pipelines
* secondary verification

---

## Human-in-the-Loop

Critical for high-stakes systems.

---

# Hallucinations Cannot Be Fully Eliminated

Current LLM systems are fundamentally probabilistic.

This means:

```text
hallucinations can be reduced
but not fully eliminated
```

The goal is usually:

* minimize frequency
* reduce severity
* improve grounding
* improve detectability

Future scientific RAG systems should prioritize:

* grounding
* traceability

---

# Important Insight

Hallucinations are not simply:

```text
"the model lying"
```

They emerge because:

* language models optimize plausibility
* retrieval may fail
* context may be incomplete
* prompting may be weak

Modern AI systems require:

```text
retrieval
+
grounding
+
evaluation
+
verification
```

to become more reliable.

---

# Key Insight

A fluent answer is not necessarily a true answer.

Reliable RAG systems depend heavily on:

* strong retrieval
* grounded prompting
* clean context
* evidence verification
* careful evaluation

Hallucination reduction is one of the central goals of modern AI systems engineering.
